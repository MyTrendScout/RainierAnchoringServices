Florida Donation Bin Anchoring Law: What Property Owners Need to Know

August 17, 2026 · Statewide Florida

If there's a donation or clothing collection bin sitting in your shopping center parking lot, church lot, or commercial property, there's a good chance it isn't anchored the way Florida law requires. Most weren't installed by anyone thinking about wind load — they were dropped off, and that's the last anyone checked.

That gap creates real exposure for the property owner, not just the organization that placed the bin.

Why This Applies to You, Not Just the Bin Operator

Collection bins are typically placed by a third-party charity or for-profit collection operator, but they sit on someone else's property. When a storm turns an unanchored steel bin into a projectile, the question of who's liable — the property owner, the bin operator, or both — is exactly the kind of gray area insurers and attorneys look for. Florida's wind-loading requirements exist precisely because loose structures like these cause real, documented damage during hurricane season.

Local ordinances in many Florida counties and municipalities specifically address unattended donation bins, often requiring a permit, contact information posted on the unit, and — critically — secure anchoring to the ground or pavement. Bins found in violation are subject to fines, and in some jurisdictions, removal at the property owner's expense.

What "Properly Anchored" Actually Means

Anchoring a collection bin correctly isn't a matter of weighing it down with cinder blocks or chaining it to a light pole. Code-compliant anchoring typically involves:

  • Ground or slab anchors rated for the bin's wind-exposed surface area
  • Anchoring hardware appropriate to the surface — asphalt, concrete, or soil each require a different approach
  • Documentation showing the installation meets local wind-load requirements, which matters if the property is ever inspected or a claim is filed

This is the kind of work that looks simple until it's done wrong — and wrong is expensive, both in fines and in what happens if that bin goes airborne in a storm.

Who's Responsible for Fixing It

In practice, most bin operators don't proactively anchor their units to code — it's not their property, and it's rarely their first priority. That leaves property owners in the position of either chasing down the operator, doing nothing and hoping for the best, or bringing in a licensed contractor to handle it directly, regardless of who placed the bin.

The third option is the only one that actually closes the liability gap. A licensed general contractor can anchor an existing bin to code, document the work, and give the property owner something to point to if it's ever questioned — by an inspector, an insurer, or a court.

What to Do If You Have Bins on Your Property

Walk your property and take stock of any collection bins currently on site. For each one, ask: is it anchored, and can anyone prove it? If the honest answer is "probably not," that's a fixable problem, typically in a single visit.
