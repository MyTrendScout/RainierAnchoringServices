Mobile Home Tie-Downs in Tallahassee, FL: Why Inland Doesn't Mean Safe

October 20, 2026 · Tallahassee & Leon County

There's a belief that holds on in Tallahassee: we're twenty-some miles from the Gulf, so hurricanes are a coastal problem. Recent history says otherwise. Hurricane Hermine in 2016, Michael in 2018 and Idalia in 2023 all pushed damaging wind well inland across the Big Bend and into Leon County, bringing down trees, power lines and poorly secured structures far from any beach.

Manufactured homes are the most exposed structures in that equation. They are light relative to their surface area, and they rely entirely on their tie-down system to stay put when wind gets underneath and around them. In Leon, Wakulla, Gadsden and Jefferson counties, plenty of those systems were installed years ago and haven't been looked at since.

Why Inland Wind Still Moves Mobile Homes

A storm weakens as it moves over land, but it doesn't weaken instantly. A strong hurricane making landfall along the Big Bend coast can still carry sustained tropical-storm or hurricane-force winds, plus stronger gusts, well into North Florida. Inland areas also deal with a few hazards the coast talks about less:

  • Falling trees. Tallahassee's canopy is one of its best features and one of its biggest storm risks. A home that shifts even a few inches off its piers is far more vulnerable when a limb comes down.
  • Long-duration wind. Slower storms can batter the same area for hours. Repeated loading works loose anchors further out of the ground with each gust.
  • Saturated ground. Heavy rain ahead of and during a storm softens soil, cutting the holding power of auger anchors right when it's needed most.

Soil Matters More Than Most People Think

The Tallahassee area doesn't have one soil type. Parts of Leon County sit on red clay hills; areas to the south toward Wakulla trend sandier and wetter. An anchor that holds well in compact clay may not hold the same way in loose sand, and vice versa. Anchor type and installation depth have to match what's actually under the home, not what was convenient to install the day it was set.

That's one of the most common problems we find: a generic anchor installed without regard to the lot's soil conditions, then left in place for decades.

What a Proper Tie-Down System Includes

Florida sets specific standards for manufactured home anchoring, and those standards have been tightened over the years. A system that meets current expectations generally includes:

  • Enough ground anchors, properly spaced, for the home's length and width
  • Anchors rated for and installed into the soil conditions on that lot
  • Frame ties, and over-the-top ties where the home's design calls for them
  • Galvanized straps and hardware in good condition, under proper tension
  • Stabilizer plates or equivalent where the anchor design requires them
  • Separate anchoring for attached carports, porches and add-ons

If any of those pieces is missing, rusted, slack or backed out of the ground, the system is weaker than it looks from the yard.

Warning Signs to Check Now

You don't need to crawl under the home to spot most red flags. Walk the perimeter and look for:

  • Straps with visible rust, fraying, or no tension
  • Anchor heads sitting above grade or tilted toward the home
  • Skirting that's buckled or pulled away, which can signal movement
  • Doors or windows that have started sticking, another movement indicator
  • An addition, carport or deck that clearly isn't tied down on its own

This Is Licensed Contractor Work

Tie-down work attracts plenty of operators with a truck and a box of straps. The trouble is that correct anchoring is engineering-driven: anchor count, anchor type, strap configuration and installation depth all depend on the home and the ground it sits on. Getting it wrong doesn't show up until the wind does.

Timing: Use the Back Half of the Season

Late October is still hurricane season, and the Gulf can stay warm enough to support storms into November. More importantly, the months right after season ends are the easiest time to schedule corrective work: no forecast cone, no supply shortages, no waiting list. Homeowners who address anchoring between seasons go into next June already done.
